Lecture 10

Solvable and nilpotent groups

We find the quotient through which all homomorphisms to abelian groups factor. Repeating the construction on successive subgroups tests solvability. We compare this with a second test that builds up from the trivial subgroup by taking centres of quotients.

Where we are

A homomorphism from GGG factors through G/NG/NG/N if N⊴GN\nsub GN⊴G lies in its kernel (Theorem 4.6). A normal subgroup of a quotient lifts to a normal subgroup of the original group (Theorem 4.8). Nontrivial ppp-groups have nontrivial centre (Theorem 7.7), and A5\AG{5}A5​ is non-abelian and simple (Theorem 9.5 and Example 9.6).

10.1 Commutators and the abelianisation

If the images of xxx and yyy are to commute in a quotient, then xyxyxy and yxyxyx must give the same coset. The product of xyxyxy with the inverse of yxyxyx records what the quotient must remove.

Definition 10.1 (Commutator and commutator subgroup). The commutator of x,y∈Gx,y\in Gx,y∈G is [x,y]=xyx−1y−1.\gcomm{x}{y}=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}.[x,y]=xyx−1y−1. The commutator subgroup G′G'G′ is generated by all these commutators.

The commutator is eee exactly when xy=yxxy=yxxy=yx, so G′=1G'=\trivG′=1 exactly when GGG is abelian.1

In S3\SG{3}S3​, take the two transpositions (1 3)(1\,3)(13) and (1 2)(1\,2)(12). Each is its own inverse. The product (1 3)(1 2)(1\,3)(1\,2)(13)(12) takes 111 to 222, 222 to 333 and 333 to 111. Hence [(1 3),(1 2)]=((1 3)(1 2))2=(1 3 2).\gcomm{(1\,3)}{(1\,2)} =\bigl((1\,3)(1\,2)\bigr)^2=(1\,3\,2).[(13),(12)]=((13)(12))2=(132). We will use this commutator to find all of S3′\SG{3}'S3′​.

For a homomorphism φ\varphiφ and a subset SSS of its domain, φ(⟨S⟩)=⟨φ(S)⟩.\begin{equation} \label{eq:10:generated-image} \varphi(\langle S\rangle)=\langle\varphi(S)\rangle. \end{equation}φ(⟨S⟩)=⟨φ(S)⟩.(10.1) Indeed, ⟨S⟩\langle S\rangle⟨S⟩ consists of products of elements of SSS and their inverses (Proposition 1.5), and taking their images gives exactly the corresponding products of images and inverses.

Conjugation preserves products and inverses. Therefore conjugating [x,y]\gcomm{x}{y}[x,y] by ggg gives the commutator of gxg−1gxg^{-1}gxg−1 and gyg−1gyg^{-1}gyg−1. Conjugation permutes the generating set and hence preserves G′G'G′ by Equation (10.1).

A quotient by G′G'G′ identifies xyxyxy with yxyxyx for every pair. We give this particular quotient a name before comparing it with the other abelian quotients.

Definition 10.2 (Abelianisation). The abelianisation of GGG is the quotient G/G′G/G'G/G′.

Proposition 10.3 (The abelianisation). Every homomorphism φ\varphiφ preserves commutators, and G′⊴GG'\nsub GG′⊴G. For N⊴GN\nsub GN⊴G, the quotient G/NG/NG/N is abelian exactly when G′≤NG'\le NG′≤N. The group G/G′G/G'G/G′ is abelian, and every homomorphism from GGG to an abelian group factors uniquely through it.

Proof. We track what happens to commutators under a homomorphism, then apply the calculation to quotient maps and to maps into abelian groups.

  1. Images of commutators. Preservation of products and inverses gives φ(xyx−1y−1)=φ(x)φ(y)φ(x)−1φ(y)−1=[φ(x),φ(y)].\varphi(xyx^{-1}y^{-1}) =\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\varphi(x)^{-1}\varphi(y)^{-1} =\gcomm{\varphi(x)}{\varphi(y)}.φ(xyx−1y−1)=φ(x)φ(y)φ(x)−1φ(y)−1=[φ(x),φ(y)]. For conjugation this is the calculation above proving normality of G′G'G′.

  2. Abelian quotients. Let q ⁣:G→G/Nq\colon G\to G/Nq:G→G/N be the quotient map. Its images q(x)q(x)q(x) and q(y)q(y)q(y) commute exactly when q([x,y])=Nq(\gcomm{x}{y})=Nq([x,y])=N, or equivalently [x,y]∈N\gcomm{x}{y}\in N[x,y]∈N. Every pair of elements of G/NG/NG/N arises in this way. The quotient is therefore abelian exactly when NNN contains all commutators, equivalently when G′≤NG'\le NG′≤N.

  3. Factorisation. The preceding criterion with N=G′N=G'N=G′ makes G/G′G/G'G/G′ abelian. If φ\varphiφ maps into an abelian group, it kills each commutator by the first step. Thus G′≤ker⁡φG'\le\ker\varphiG′≤kerφ and Theorem 4.6 gives the unique factorisation.

 

Example 10.4 (The commutator subgroup of S3\SG{3}S3​). The sign quotient S3/A3\SG{3}/\AG{3}S3​/A3​ has order two and is abelian. The quotient criterion puts S3′\SG{3}'S3′​ inside A3\AG{3}A3​. The commutator (1 3 2)(1\,3\,2)(132) computed above generates A3\AG{3}A3​. The two inclusions give S3′=A3\SG{3}'=\AG{3}S3′​=A3​ and the abelianisation is cyclic of order two. Each homomorphism from S3\SG{3}S3​ to an abelian group therefore factors through the sign.

An abelian quotient G/NG/NG/N is itself a quotient of G/G′G/G'G/G′. Indeed its quotient map from GGG factors through G/G′G/G'G/G′, and the induced map is onto. In particular, factorisation and composition with the quotient map give inverse bijections between the homomorphisms G→C×G\to\C^\timesG→C× and G/G′→C×G/G'\to\C^\timesG/G′→C×. We use this correspondence to describe how S4\SG{4}S4​ can act by nonzero scalars in §14.4.

10.2 Derived series and solvability

The commutator subgroup can itself be non-abelian. We can take its commutator subgroup and continue. If the sequence reaches 1\triv1, the successive quotients express the group in terms of abelian groups.

Definition 10.5 (Derived series and solvability). The derived series of GGG is defined by G(0)=G,G(i+1)=(G(i))′(i≥0).G^{(0)}=G,\qquad G^{(i+1)}=\bigl(G^{(i)}\bigr)' \quad (i\ge0).G(0)=G,G(i+1)=(G(i))′(i≥0). The group GGG is solvable if G(m)=1G^{(m)}=\trivG(m)=1 for some m≥0m\ge0m≥0.

By Proposition 10.3, each term is normal in the previous one and the quotient is abelian. Since A3\AG{3}A3​ is abelian, the calculation for S3\SG{3}S3​ gives the series S3⊵A3⊵1\SG{3}\nsup\AG{3}\nsup\trivS3​⊵A3​⊵1. In particular, S3\SG{3}S3​ is solvable.

The derived series of S4\SG{4}S4​.

We compute the first two steps using the same pair of inclusions as in the calculation for S3\SG{3}S3​. To identify a normal subgroup from the commutators it contains, use the lists for S4\SG{4}S4​ and A4\AG{4}A4​ in §9.3.

  1. From S4\SG{4}S4​ to A4\AG{4}A4​. The quotient by A4\AG{4}A4​ is abelian, so S4′≤A4\SG{4}'\le\AG{4}S4′​≤A4​. The transpositions (1 3)(1\,3)(13) and (1 2)(1\,2)(12) from §10.1 act on the same three letters inside S4\SG{4}S4​. Their commutator is a 333-cycle. The only normal subgroup of S4\SG{4}S4​ inside A4\AG{4}A4​ that contains a 333-cycle is A4\AG{4}A4​ itself, so S4′=A4\SG{4}'=\AG{4}S4′​=A4​.

  2. From A4\AG{4}A4​ to V4\KlV4​. The quotient A4/V4\AG{4}/\KlA4​/V4​ has order three and is abelian, so A4′≤V4\AG{4}'\le\KlA4′​≤V4​. Both arguments of the following commutator are even. Use the conjugation table in Example 8.9, then multiply the double transpositions as in Example 5.5.   3. From V4\KlV4​ to 1\triv1. The group V4\KlV4​ is abelian, so V4′=1\Kl'=\trivV4′​=1. The full series is S4⊵A4⊵V4⊵1.\SG{4}\nsup\AG{4}\nsup\Kl\nsup\triv.S4​⊵A4​⊵V4​⊵1. Its factors are C2\Cyc{2}C2​, C3\Cyc{3}C3​ and V4≅C2×C2\Kl\cong\Cyc{2}\times\Cyc{2}V4​≅C2​×C2​. The final abelian factor can be split further in a composition series (§9.3).

The computation shows that a short series with abelian factors suffices for S4\SG{4}S4​. We next check how such series behave when we pass to a subgroup or a quotient, or when we join a normal subgroup to its quotient.

Theorem 10.6 (Solvable groups).

  1. If H≤GH\le GH≤G, then H(i)≤G(i)H^{(i)}\le G^{(i)}H(i)≤G(i) for each i≥0i\ge0i≥0. In particular, subgroups of solvable groups are solvable.

  2. If φ ⁣:G→K\varphi\colon G\to Kφ:G→K is onto, then φ(G(i))=K(i)\varphi(G^{(i)})=K^{(i)}φ(G(i))=K(i) for each i≥0i\ge0i≥0. In particular, quotients of solvable groups are solvable.

  3. If N⊴GN\nsub GN⊴G and NNN and G/NG/NG/N are solvable, then GGG is solvable.

  4. A group GGG is solvable exactly when it admits a finite chain G=B0⊵B1⊵⋯⊵Bℓ=1G=B_0\nsup B_1\nsup\dots\nsup B_\ell=\trivG=B0​⊵B1​⊵⋯⊵Bℓ​=1 with each Bi+1B_{i+1}Bi+1​ normal in BiB_iBi​ and every quotient Bi/Bi+1B_i/B_{i+1}Bi​/Bi+1​ abelian.

Proof. We first compare the derived series under inclusions and surjections. Then we use these comparisons to bound how far the series can go.

  1. Subgroups. Start with H≤GH\le GH≤G. If H(i)≤G(i)H^{(i)}\le G^{(i)}H(i)≤G(i), then each commutator of elements of H(i)H^{(i)}H(i) is a commutator of elements of G(i)G^{(i)}G(i). This gives the inclusion for the next step.

  2. Surjections. Start with φ(G)=K\varphi(G)=Kφ(G)=K. If φ(G(i))=K(i)\varphi(G^{(i)})=K^{(i)}φ(G(i))=K(i), every pair in K(i)K^{(i)}K(i) has lifts in G(i)G^{(i)}G(i). By Proposition 10.3, the commutator of those lifts maps to the commutator of the pair. Thus the generating commutators map onto the generating commutators at the next step, and Equation (10.1) gives φ(G(i+1))=K(i+1)\varphi(G^{(i+1)})=K^{(i+1)}φ(G(i+1))=K(i+1).

  3. A normal subgroup and its quotient. If (G/N)(n)=1(G/N)^{(n)}=\triv(G/N)(n)=1, the surjection property puts G(n)G^{(n)}G(n) inside the kernel NNN. If N(m)=1N^{(m)}=\trivN(m)=1, the subgroup property now gives G(n+m)=(G(n))(m)≤N(m)=1.\begin{aligned} G^{(n+m)} &= (G^{(n)})^{(m)}\\ &\le N^{(m)}\\ &=\triv. \end{aligned}G(n+m)​=(G(n))(m)≤N(m)=1.​

  4. An abelian-factor chain. If GGG is solvable, use its derived series up to 1\triv1. Conversely, suppose the chain in the statement is given. Start from G(0)=B0G^{(0)}=B_0G(0)=B0​. If G(i)≤BiG^{(i)}\le B_iG(i)≤Bi​, the subgroup property and the abelian quotient criterion give G(i+1)=(G(i))′≤Bi′by the subgroup property,≤Bi+1since Bi/Bi+1 is abelian.\begin{aligned} G^{(i+1)} &= (G^{(i)})'\\ &\le B_i' &&\text{by the subgroup property},\\ &\le B_{i+1} &&\text{since }B_i/B_{i+1}\text{ is abelian}. \end{aligned}G(i+1)​=(G(i))′≤Bi′​≤Bi+1​​​by the subgroup property,since Bi​/Bi+1​ is abelian.​ By induction G(ℓ)≤Bℓ=1G^{(\ell)}\le B_\ell=\trivG(ℓ)≤Bℓ​=1.

 

When N⊴GN\nsub GN⊴G, we call GGG an extension of G/NG/NG/N by NNN. For example, π ⁣:S4→S3\pi\colon\SG{4}\to\SG{3}π:S4​→S3​ is onto with kernel V4\KlV4​ (§5.4). The surjection property gives π(A4)=A3\pi(\AG{4})=\AG{3}π(A4​)=A3​ and π(V4)=1\pi(\Kl)=\trivπ(V4​)=1 by comparing the two series. Since S3\SG{3}S3​ and V4\KlV4​ are solvable, the extension property also gives solvability of S4\SG{4}S4​. The same conclusion follows from the composition series in §9.3, whose factors are all abelian.

A non-abelian simple group GGG has a nontrivial normal commutator subgroup, so G′=GG'=GG′=G and the series stays at GGG. In particular A5\AG{5}A5​ is not solvable.

Corollary 10.7 (Solvable symmetric groups). The group Sn\SG{n}Sn​ is solvable exactly when n≤4n\le4n≤4.

Proof. For n≤4n\le4n≤4, extend permutations to four letters by fixing the remaining points. Thus Sn\SG{n}Sn​ is isomorphic to a subgroup of the solvable group S4\SG{4}S4​ and is solvable by Theorem 10.6. For n≥5n\ge5n≥5, the even permutations on the first five letters form a copy of the non-solvable group A5\AG{5}A5​. A solvable group cannot have such a subgroup.

10.3 Nilpotent groups and ppp-groups

The derived series starts with the whole group and takes smaller subgroups. We can instead start at 1\triv1 and enlarge it by putting in the centre. The next enlargement uses elements whose cosets are central in the quotient by the subgroup already reached.

Definition 10.8 (Upper central series and nilpotence). The upper central series of GGG starts at Z0=1Z_0=\trivZ0​=1. For i≥0i\ge0i≥0, the subgroup Zi+1Z_{i+1}Zi+1​ is the preimage of Z(G/Zi)\Zent{G/Z_i}Z(G/Zi​) under the quotient map, so Zi+1/Zi=Z(G/Zi).Z_{i+1}/Z_i=\Zent{G/Z_i}.Zi+1​/Zi​=Z(G/Zi​). The group GGG is nilpotent if Zm=GZ_m=GZm​=G for some m≥0m\ge0m≥0.

The centre is normal. Thus if Zi⊴GZ_i\nsub GZi​⊴G, its successor is normal by Theorem 4.8. This justifies each new quotient. The terms increase because each preimage contains the kernel ZiZ_iZi​. The first term after 1\triv1 is Z1=Z(G)Z_1=\Zent GZ1​=Z(G). For C6\Cyc{6}C6​, the centre is the whole group, so the series reaches it in one step.

Theorem 10.9 (Nilpotent groups). Every finite ppp-group is nilpotent. Every nilpotent group is solvable.

Proof. For a ppp-group we show that the upper central series grows until it fills the group. Then we read the same series in the reverse direction to obtain abelian factors.

  1. Growth for ppp-groups. Let ∣P∣=pa\abs P=p^a∣P∣=pa. If Zi≠PZ_i\ne PZi​=P, the quotient P/ZiP/Z_iP/Zi​ is a nontrivial ppp-group. By Theorem 7.7, its centre has at least ppp elements. The defining quotient for Zi+1Z_{i+1}Zi+1​ then gives ∣Zi+1∣=∣Zi∣ ∣Z(P/Zi)∣≥p∣Zi∣.\abs{Z_{i+1}} =\abs{Z_i}\,\abs{\Zent{P/Z_i}} \ge p\abs{Z_i}.∣Zi+1​∣=∣Zi​∣∣Z(P/Zi​)∣≥p∣Zi​∣. If Za<PZ_a<PZa​<P, then all the terms up to ZaZ_aZa​ are proper. Starting with ∣Z0∣=1\abs{Z_0}=1∣Z0​∣=1, the growth inequality gives ∣Za∣≥pa=∣P∣\abs{Z_a}\ge p^a=\abs P∣Za​∣≥pa=∣P∣, a contradiction. Thus Za=PZ_a=PZa​=P.

  2. Abelian factors. Now let GGG be nilpotent with Zm=GZ_m=GZm​=G. Read its series from ZmZ_mZm​ down to Z0Z_0Z0​. Each quotient Zi+1/ZiZ_{i+1}/Z_iZi+1​/Zi​ is the centre of a group and hence abelian. This is an abelian-factor chain, so Theorem 10.6 gives solvability.

 

Example 10.10 (Guard: solvable need not imply nilpotent). The group S3\SG{3}S3​ is solvable, with derived subgroup A3\AG{3}A3​ of prime order. Its centre is trivial by Example 3.2. Summary

  • For N⊴GN\nsub GN⊴G, the quotient G/NG/NG/N is abelian exactly when G′≤NG'\le NG′≤N. Every homomorphism from GGG to an abelian group factors through G/G′G/G'G/G′ (Proposition 10.3).
  • A solvable group has a derived series reaching 1\triv1. Solvability is inherited by subgroups and quotients and passes to extensions (Theorem 10.6). The group Sn\SG{n}Sn​ is solvable exactly for n≤4n\le4n≤4 (Corollary 10.7).
  • The upper central series of a nilpotent group reaches the whole group. Every finite ppp-group is nilpotent, and every nilpotent group is solvable (Theorem 10.9).
