Lecture 21

Tensor constructions and the Frobenius–Schur indicator

We build duals and tensor products from representations we know. Splitting a tensor square lets us count invariant bilinear forms. The resulting test uses character values at squares and distinguishes two planes whose groups have the same character table.

Where we are

The vector-space tensor product has basis va⊗wbv_a\otimes w_bva​⊗wb​ for bases of the factors, and bilinear maps on the factors extend linearly (Definition 16.3). The twist by a line multiplies each operator by the scalar on that line (Proposition 14.8). We extend this construction to two arbitrary finite-dimensional complex representations of a finite group GGG.

21.1 Duals and tensor squares

A functional on VVV can follow the action on VVV by testing a vector after moving it back. A tensor of two vectors can follow the two actions together. These give two ways to build representations without choosing bases.

When both factors are VVV, exchanging them makes sense. Adding a tensor to its exchange or subtracting the exchange gives two pieces that transform separately.

21.2 Invariant bilinear forms

Example 12.3 and §12.4 raised the question of when a complex irreducible has real matrices in some basis. If all matrices D(g)D(g)D(g) are real in one basis, define B(v,v′)=∑g∈G(D(g)v)TD(g)v′.B(v,v')=\sum_{g\in G}(D(g)v)^{\mathsf T}D(g)v'.B(v,v′)=g∈G∑​(D(g)v)TD(g)v′. The form is symmetric, and acting by any group element on both inputs reindexes the sum. For a nonzero real coordinate vector vvv, all summands of B(v,v)B(v,v)B(v,v) are nonnegative and the identity summand is positive. A test for a nonzero invariant symmetric form is therefore a first test for real matrices.

21.3 The quaternion group and two equal tables

The indicator needs the class of each square. To see what changes when the class sizes and the character values agree, we compare the square group with a second group of order eight.

Summary

  • Duals conjugate characters, and tensor products multiply them. The two squares use both χV(g)2\chi_V(g)^2χV​(g)2 and χV(g2)\chi_V(g^2)χV​(g2) (Theorem 21.4).
  • For an irreducible, ν=1,−1,0\FS=1,-1,0ν=1,−1,0 gives a nonzero symmetric form, a nonzero alternating form, or only the zero form, respectively (Theorem 21.6).
  • The square and quaternion planes have the same character row in equal tables. Their square maps give different indicators.
  • Lecture 22 begins the study of symmetric-group irreducibles by restriction. The Category Theory course returns to the Frobenius–Schur indicator.

3. Kernels in the two groups.

The kernels are the elements with character equal to 111 (Proposition 20.2). The corresponding classes give {e,r2,rs,r3s}\{e,r^2,rs,r^3s\}{e,r2,rs,r3s} and {±1,±k}\{\pm1,\pm k\}{±1,±k}, respectively. The three nonidentity elements of the first kernel have order two, so the kernel is V4\KlV4​. The element kkk generates the second kernel, which is cyclic of order four. The same linear row locates the kernel classes in both groups, and their squares distinguish the kernels.
