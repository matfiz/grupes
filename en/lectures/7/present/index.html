Lecture 7

Cauchy, ppp-groups and Sylow existence

Lagrange’s theorem restricts the orders a subgroup can have. We prove that every prime power dividing the order of a finite group occurs as a subgroup order. We start by counting fixed points to find elements of prime order.

Where we are

An orbit has as many points as the index of a stabiliser (Theorem 2.9). For conjugation this gives the size of a class as the index of a centraliser (Theorem 3.3). Subgroups above a normal subgroup correspond to subgroups of the quotient, with the same indices and normality (Theorem 4.8). The class and sign table of S4\SG{4}S4​ identifies A4\AG{4}A4​ and its order (§6.4).

7.1 Fixed points and Cauchy’s theorem

A point fixed by every group element is an orbit by itself. These points can be counted separately from the larger orbits.

Definition 7.1 (Common fixed set). For an action of GGG on XXX, the set of points fixed by all of GGG is XG={x∈X:g⋅x=x for all g∈G}.X^G=\{x\in X:g\cdot x=x\text{ for all }g\in G\}.XG={x∈X:g⋅x=x for all g∈G}.

This is the intersection of the sets Fix⁡(g)\Fix(g)Fix(g) for individual elements. On the triangle the subgroup ⟨s⟩\langle s\rangle⟨s⟩ fixes vertex 111, so its common fixed set is {1}\{1\}{1}. Its other orbit has the two points {2,3}\{2,3\}{2,3} (Example 2.3).

Recall that a≡b(modp)a\equiv b\pmod pa≡b(modp) means that ppp divides a−ba-ba−b (Example 1.10). For the action just described, 3≡1(mod2)3\equiv 1\pmod 23≡1(mod2). The two points outside the common fixed set contribute a multiple of two.

Lemma 7.2 (Fixed points modulo ppp). Let ppp be prime. If a group GGG of order pap^apa, with a≥0a\ge0a≥0 an integer, acts on a finite set XXX, then ∣X∣≡∣XG∣(modp)\abs{X}\equiv\abs{X^G}\pmod p∣X∣≡​XG​(modp).

Proof. The orbits partition XXX. Each orbit size divides ∣G∣\abs G∣G∣ by orbit–stabiliser and Lagrange’s theorem (Theorem 2.9 and Corollary 2.11). Each orbit therefore has size one or a multiple of ppp. The orbits of size one contribute exactly ∣XG∣\abs{X^G}​XG​ to the sum of the orbit sizes. 

Lagrange’s theorem gives a necessary condition on the order of an element (Corollary 2.12). For prime divisors we can also find an element. We look for it among the constant tuples whose product is eee.

Theorem 7.4 (Cauchy). If GGG is finite and the prime ppp divides ∣G∣\abs G∣G∣, then GGG has an element of order ppp.

Proof. We count tuples of product eee, let a cyclic group shift their entries, and read the order of an element from a fixed tuple. This is McKay’s proof.

  1. Count the tuples. Let XXX be the set of (g1,…,gp)∈Gp(g_1,\dots,g_p)\in G^p(g1​,…,gp​)∈Gp with product eee. Choose the first p−1p-1p−1 entries freely. The last is (g1⋯gp−1)−1(g_1\cdots g_{p-1})^{-1}(g1​⋯gp−1​)−1. Thus ∣X∣=∣G∣p−1\abs{X}=\abs G^{p-1}∣X∣=∣G∣p−1 is divisible by ppp.

  2. Shift the entries. Moving the last entry to the front conjugates the product by gpg_pgp​. In particular, gpg1⋯gp−1=gp(g1⋯gp)gp−1=e.g_pg_1\cdots g_{p-1}=g_p(g_1\cdots g_p)g_p^{-1}=e.gp​g1​⋯gp−1​=gp​(g1​⋯gp​)gp−1​=e. Let a generator ggg of Cp\Cyc{p}Cp​ act by this shift. After ppp shifts every entry returns to its place. The action of gkg^kgk by kkk shifts is well defined and respects products since successive shifts add their exponents modulo ppp.

  3. Read the fixed tuples. A tuple fixed by the generator is fixed by the whole cyclic group. Its entries agree, so XCp={(x,…,x):x∈G, xp=e}.X^{\Cyc{p}}=\{(x,\dots,x):x\in G,\ x^p=e\}.XCp​={(x,…,x):x∈G, xp=e}. 7.2 Groups of prime-power order

When the whole group has prime-power order, the fixed-point congruence applies to its own action by conjugation.

Definition 7.5 (ppp-groups and ppp-subgroups). Let ppp be prime. A ppp-group has order pap^apa for some integer a≥0a\ge0a≥0. A ppp-subgroup of GGG is a subgroup of GGG that is a ppp-group.

The Klein group V4\KlV4​ has order 222^222 and is a 222-subgroup of S4\SG{4}S4​ (Example 5.5). By Lagrange’s theorem, the subgroups of a ppp-group have ppp-power order, as do its elements.

A small quotient by the centre puts a strong restriction on the group. If one coset generates that quotient, each element differs from a power of a single element by a central factor.

Lemma 7.6 (Cyclic quotient by the centre). If G/Z(G)G/\Zent{G}G/Z(G) is cyclic, then GGG is abelian.

Proof. Write Z=Z(G)Z=\Zent{G}Z=Z(G) and let gZgZgZ generate G/ZG/ZG/Z. Each coset is giZg^iZgiZ for some iii, so every element of GGG has the form gizg^izgiz with z∈Zz\in Zz∈Z. Central factors commute with all elements, and powers of ggg commute with each other. Thus (giz)(gjw)=gi+jzw=(gjw)(giz).(g^iz)(g^jw)=g^{i+j}zw=(g^jw)(g^iz). \qedhere(giz)(gjw)=gi+jzw=(gjw)(giz). 

Theorem 7.7 (ppp-groups). Let PPP have order pap^apa, where ppp is prime and a≥0a\ge0a≥0 is an integer.

  1. If a≥1a\ge1a≥1, then Z(P)≠1\Zent{P}\ne\trivZ(P)=1.

  2. For each integer bbb with 0≤b≤a0\le b\le a0≤b≤a, there is a normal subgroup of PPP of order pbp^bpb.

  3. If a=2a=2a=2, then PPP is abelian and is isomorphic to Cp2\Cyc{p^2}Cp2​ or Cp×Cp\Cyc{p}\times\Cyc{p}Cp​×Cp​.

Proof. We count the centre, pass through a central subgroup of order ppp to build normal subgroups, and then specialise to order p2p^2p2.

  1. The centre. Let PPP act on itself by conjugation. Its common fixed points are the elements of Z(P)\Zent{P}Z(P), namely those that commute with every element of PPP. By Lemma 7.2, ∣Z(P)∣≡∣P∣≡0(modp)(a≥1).\abs{\Zent{P}}\equiv\abs P\equiv0\pmod p \qquad (a\ge1).∣Z(P)∣≡∣P∣≡0(modp)(a≥1). Since eee is central, the centre has at least ppp elements.

  2. Normal subgroups. We induct on aaa. For b=0b=0b=0, including the base case a=0a=0a=0, use 1\triv1. For b≥1b\ge1b≥1, the first step gives p∣∣Z(P)∣p\mid\abs{\Zent{P}}p∣∣Z(P)∣, so Cauchy’s theorem gives a central element zzz of order ppp. Every conjugation fixes each power of zzz, so N=⟨z⟩N=\langle z\rangleN=⟨z⟩ is normal in PPP.

    The quotient P/NP/NP/N has order pa−1p^{a-1}pa−1. By induction it has a normal subgroup Kˉ\bar KKˉ of order pb−1p^{b-1}pb−1. The correspondence theorem makes its preimage KKK normal in PPP and preserves the index. Hence [P:K]=[P/N:Kˉ]=pa−b.\idx{P}{K}=\idx{P/N}{\bar K}=p^{a-b}.[P:K]=[P/N:Kˉ]=pa−b. This gives ∣K∣=pb\abs K=p^b∣K∣=pb.

  3. Order p2p^2p2. The centre has order ppp or p2p^2p2 by the first step and Lagrange. If it has order ppp, the quotient by it has prime order and is cyclic (Corollary 2.12). By Lemma 7.6, PPP is then abelian, so its centre is all of PPP, a contradiction. Thus the centre has order p2p^2p2 and PPP is abelian.

    If PPP has an element of order p2p^2p2, it generates PPP. Otherwise every nonidentity element has order ppp by Lagrange. Choose x≠ex\ne ex=e and y∉⟨x⟩y\notin\langle x\rangley∈/⟨x⟩. Both cyclic subgroups have prime order, and their intersection is proper in ⟨y⟩\langle y\rangle⟨y⟩, hence trivial. They are normal since PPP is abelian, and their orders multiply to ∣P∣\abs P∣P∣. The internal direct-product criterion (Theorem 5.3) now gives P≅⟨x⟩×⟨y⟩≅Cp×Cp.P\cong\langle x\rangle\times\langle y\rangle \cong\Cyc{p}\times\Cyc{p}. \qedhereP≅⟨x⟩×⟨y⟩≅Cp​×Cp​.

 

An element of order p2p^2p2 distinguishes Cp2\Cyc{p^2}Cp2​ from Cp×Cp\Cyc{p}\times\Cyc{p}Cp​×Cp​, since every element of the product has order dividing ppp (§5.1).

In V4\KlV4​, every nonidentity element squares to eee, so the classification gives the product of two cyclic groups of order two. The centre claim needs the prime-power hypothesis, as S3\SG{3}S3​ has trivial centre by its class sizes (Example 3.2).

7.3 Sylow existence

Write ∣G∣=pam\abs G=p^a m∣G∣=pam with a≥0a\ge0a≥0 and p∤mp\nmid mp∤m. The largest order Lagrange allows for a ppp-subgroup of GGG is pap^apa.

Definition 7.9 (Sylow subgroup). A Sylow ppp-subgroup of GGG is a subgroup of order pap^apa. Write Syl⁡p(G)\Syl{p}(G)Sylp​(G) for the set of these subgroups and np=∣Syl⁡p(G)∣n_p=\abs{\Syl{p}(G)}np​=​Sylp​(G)​ for their number.

In S3\SG{3}S3​, A3=⟨(1 2 3)⟩\AG{3}=\langle(1\,2\,3)\rangleA3​=⟨(123)⟩ is a Sylow 333-subgroup and ⟨(1 2)⟩\langle(1\,2)\rangle⟨(12)⟩ is a Sylow 222-subgroup. If a=0a=0a=0, the trivial subgroup is the only Sylow ppp-subgroup.

(a) The proper-subgroup step on S4\SG{4}S4​, p=2p=2p=2.

The subgroup H1H_1H1​ has odd index three, so a Sylow 222-subgroup of H1H_1H1​ is also one of S4\SG{4}S4​. Since ∣H1∣=8\abs{H_1}=8∣H1​∣=8, H1H_1H1​ itself is Sylow.

Thus Z(D6)=⟨r3⟩\Zent{\Dih{6}}=\langle r^3\rangleZ(D6​)=⟨r3⟩ has order two.

The quotient D6/⟨r3⟩\Dih{6}/\langle r^3\rangleD6​/⟨r3⟩ has order six. The coset of sss has order two and generates a Sylow 222-subgroup there. Its preimage is ⟨r3,s⟩={e,r3,s,r3s},\langle r^3,s\rangle=\{e,r^3,s,r^3s\},⟨r3,s⟩={e,r3,s,r3s}, of order four. The lift works whenever ppp divides ∣Z(G)∣\abs{\Zent G}∣Z(G)∣. Theorem 7.10 (Sylow existence). Every finite group has a Sylow ppp-subgroup for each prime ppp.

Proof. We induct on ∣G∣\abs G∣G∣. A proper subgroup can keep the full ppp-power in ∣G∣\abs G∣G∣. When there is no such proper subgroup, the class equation supplies a central subgroup to divide out.

  1. The base and the proper-subgroup case. For a=0a=0a=0 use 1\triv1. This also covers the group of order one. Suppose a≥1a\ge1a≥1. If H<GH<GH<G has p∤[G:H]p\nmid\idx{G}{H}p∤[G:H], then Lagrange gives ∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣\abs G=\idx{G}{H}\abs H∣G∣=[G:H]∣H∣, so the largest power of ppp dividing ∣H∣\abs H∣H∣ is pap^apa. Apply induction to HHH to find a subgroup of that order.

  2. Find a central element. Suppose every proper subgroup has index divisible by ppp. For each representative xxx of a noncentral class, CG(x)\Cent{G}{x}CG​(x) is proper, so its index is divisible by ppp by hypothesis. Also p∣∣G∣p\mid\abs Gp∣∣G∣ since a≥1a\ge1a≥1. The class equation (Theorem 3.3) gives ∣G∣=∣Z(G)∣+∑x[G:CG(x)],∣G∣≡∣Z(G)∣(modp),∣Z(G)∣≡0(modp).\begin{aligned} \abs G &= \abs{\Zent{G}}+\sum_x\idx{G}{\Cent{G}{x}},\\ \abs G &\equiv \abs{\Zent{G}}\pmod p,\\ \abs{\Zent{G}} &\equiv0\pmod p. \end{aligned}∣G∣∣G∣∣Z(G)∣​=∣Z(G)∣+x∑​[G:CG​(x)],≡∣Z(G)∣(modp),≡0(modp).​ Cauchy’s theorem applied to Z(G)\Zent{G}Z(G) gives zzz of order ppp, and N=⟨z⟩N=\langle z\rangleN=⟨z⟩ is normal because it is central.

  3. Lift from the quotient. The group G/NG/NG/N has order pa−1mp^{a-1}mpa−1m. By induction it has a subgroup Kˉ\bar KKˉ of order pa−1p^{a-1}pa−1. When a=1a=1a=1 this subgroup is 1\triv1. Its preimage KKK has the same index mmm by Theorem 4.8, so ∣K∣=pa\abs K=p^a∣K∣=pa.

 

Once existence is known, branch (2) can occur only for ppp-groups because in any other group a Sylow ppp-subgroup is proper and has index prime to ppp, which is an argument that requires the existence just proved.

Corollary 7.11 (Prime-power converse to Lagrange). Let GGG be finite, ppp prime and b≥0b\ge0b≥0 an integer. If pbp^bpb divides ∣G∣\abs G∣G∣, then GGG has a subgroup of order pbp^bpb.

Proof. A Sylow ppp-subgroup has order pap^apa with a≥ba\ge ba≥b. By Theorem 7.7, it has a subgroup of order pbp^bpb, normal in the Sylow subgroup.

7.4 Worked example: the prime-power divisors of ∣A4∣\abs{\AG{4}}∣A4​∣

The possible nontrivial prime-power orders here are 222, 333 and 444, since ∣A4∣=12\abs{\AG{4}}=12∣A4​∣=12 by Theorem 6.7. We find a subgroup for each.

Order four.

The subgroup V4\KlV4​ has odd index three in A4\AG{4}A4​, so it gives the proper-subgroup branch of Theorem 7.10 at p=2p=2p=2. Its elements are even by the S4\SG{4}S4​ class table (§6.4). The full power of two is already in V4\KlV4​, which is itself a Sylow 222-subgroup.

Order two.

By Corollary 7.11 we can find the smaller power of two inside V4\KlV4​. Take ⟨(1 2)(3 4)⟩\langle(1\,2)(3\,4)\rangle⟨(12)(34)⟩, generated by an element that squares to eee. This subgroup is normal in V4\KlV4​, as predicted by Theorem 7.7. Conjugation by (1 2 3)(1\,2\,3)(123) sends its generator to (1 4)(2 3)(1\,4)(2\,3)(14)(23) by the relabelling formula (Theorem 6.4). Thus normality here is only in the Sylow subgroup.

Order three.

Cauchy’s theorem guarantees an element of order three since three divides twelve. The even cycle (1 2 3)(1\,2\,3)(123) is one such element. Its powers form a subgroup of order three, the full power of three dividing twelve. Theorem 8.2 will tell us how the subgroups of this order are related.

Summary

  • For an action of a finite ppp-group, only the singleton orbits contribute to the point count modulo ppp (Lemma 7.2). Applied to the cyclic shift of tuples, this proves Cauchy’s theorem (Theorem 7.4).
  • A nontrivial ppp-group has a nontrivial centre and has a normal subgroup of each order dividing its order. At order p2p^2p2 it is abelian (Theorem 7.7).
  • A Sylow ppp-subgroup has the full ppp-power order allowed by Lagrange. It exists by Theorem 7.10, and contains subgroups of all the smaller ppp-power orders (Corollary 7.11).
