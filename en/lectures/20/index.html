What this lecture does

We reach the group-theoretic destination named in Lecture 11: Burnside’s theorem on the solvability of groups whose orders have at most two prime divisors. The proof uses a character value to find a proper nontrivial normal subgroup. We begin by examining what a single character value can tell us about its representing operator.

Where we are

We use finite-dimensional complex representations of a finite group GGG. Each operator ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) is diagonalisable with roots of unity as eigenvalues (Corollary 12.6). Character values and the scalars of class sums on irreducibles are algebraic integers (Theorems 19.4 and 19.11). The roots of a minimal polynomial are the algebraic conjugates of its defining number (Definition 19.6).

20.1 The size of character values

The character value χ(g)\chi(g)χ(g) is a sum of eigenvalues on the unit circle. We determine when its modulus is as large as possible and what χ\chiχ tells us about the kernel.

Definition 20.1 (Kernel of a character). For a character χ\chiχ of GGG, its kernel is ker⁡χ={g∈G:χ(g)=χ(e)}.\ker\chi=\{g\in G:\chi(g)=\chi(e)\}.kerχ={g∈G:χ(g)=χ(e)}.

On the trivial line every trace is 111, so its character has kernel GGG.

The trace records the sum of eigenvalues. At the extreme of its possible modulus, that sum can also tell us what the entire operator does.

20.3 Burnside’s theorem

A noncentral element has a conjugacy class with more than one element. When its size is a prime power, we can find a nontrivial irreducible in which the element acts as a scalar. That scalar action forces a nontrivial kernel, which we use in an induction on the group order.

Theorem 20.6 (Burnside).

Summary
