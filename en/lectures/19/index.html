Part II — Representations and the Symmetric Group

Algebraic integers and the degrees of irreducibles

What this lecture does

A character value can involve roots of unity. We learn what survives of ordinary integer arithmetic for these values. Applying it to the scalars of class sums will show that the degree of every irreducible divides the order of the group.

Where we are

The eigenvalues of a representing operator are roots of unity (Corollary 12.6). We use integer polynomials to study these eigenvalues and their sums. The norm of an irreducible character is one (Corollary 13.9). The class sums are a basis of the centre of C[G]\grpalg{G}C[G] and act as scalars on each irreducible (Theorem 15.11).

19.1 Algebraic integers

If ggg has finite order, its eigenvalues are roots of unity (Corollary 12.6). Each of these is a root of a polynomial tm−1t^m-1tm−1. We retain the two features of that polynomial that make the arithmetic work: it is monic and has integer coefficients.

Definition 19.1 (Algebraic integer). A complex number α\alphaα is an algebraic integer if it is a root of a monic polynomial h(t)=tn+cn−1tn−1+⋯+c0,n≥1,cj∈Z.h(t)=t^n+c_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\dots+c_0,\qquad n\ge1,\quad c_j\in\Z.h(t)=tn+cn−1​tn−1+⋯+c0​,n≥1,cj​∈Z.

An ordinary integer aaa qualifies by t−at-at−a. The number 2\sqrt{2}2​ qualifies by t2−2t^2-2t2−2. Every root of unity qualifies by tm−1t^m-1tm−1 for its order mmm. We will show that a rational number such as 12\tfrac1221​ cannot qualify.

A polynomial gives us one way to recognise an algebraic integer. A matrix gives us another, and matrices let us combine two eigenvalues by acting on the two factors of a tensor.

Lemma 19.2 (Integer matrices). A complex number is an algebraic integer if and only if it is an eigenvalue of a square matrix with integer entries.

Proof.

  1. From a matrix to a polynomial. For an integer matrix AAA, the determinant det⁡(t11−A)\det(t\Id-A)det(t11−A) is monic with integer coefficients. Every eigenvalue of AAA is a root.

  2. From a polynomial to a matrix. For a monic hhh as in Definition 19.1, let CCC have ones just above the diagonal, last row (−c0,…,−cn−1)(-c_0,\dots,-c_{n-1})(−c0​,…,−cn−1​) and zeros elsewhere. This is the companion matrix. The construction also works with noninteger coefficients and is used for the cyclotomic polynomial in Lemma 20.5. For a root α\alphaα of hhh, take vvv with coordinates vj=αj−1v_j=\alpha^{j-1}vj​=αj−1 for 1≤j≤n1\le j\le n1≤j≤n. A character adds eigenvalues, while the degree argument will multiply character values by scalars. Both operations must preserve algebraic integrality. We also need a way to return to ordinary integers when the answer is rational.

  1. If α\alphaα and β\betaβ are algebraic integers, so are α+β\alpha+\betaα+β and αβ\alpha\betaαβ.

Definition 19.6 (Minimal polynomial and algebraic conjugates). Let α∈C\alpha\in\Cα∈C be a root of a nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients. Its minimal polynomial PPP is a monic polynomial with rational coefficients, of least degree, with P(α)=0P(\alpha)=0P(α)=0. The roots of PPP are the algebraic conjugates of α\alphaα.

This is a third use of conjugate, alongside complex conjugation and conjugacy in a group. The minimal polynomial of i\iii is t2+1t^2+1t2+1, since no rational linear polynomial vanishes at i\iii. Its algebraic conjugates are i\iii and −i-\ii−i. We need the minimal polynomial to inherit integer coefficients from any monic integer polynomial vanishing at the same number.

Lemma 19.7 (Minimal polynomials). Let PPP be the minimal polynomial of α\alphaα.

  1. PPP divides every polynomial fff with rational coefficients and f(α)=0f(\alpha)=0f(α)=0. So PPP is unique.

  2. If α≠0\alpha\ne0α=0, then P(0)≠0P(0)\ne0P(0)=0.

  3. If α\alphaα is an algebraic integer, PPP has integer coefficients.

Proof. Division proves the first two properties. For the third, we compare roots with those of a monic integer polynomial and then expand.

  1. Divide with remainder. Write f=QP+Rf=QP+Rf=QP+R. If the remainder RRR were nonzero, it would have degree less than deg⁡P\deg PdegP and would vanish at α\alphaα. Dividing it by its leading coefficient would give a monic polynomial of smaller degree, contrary to minimality. Thus PPP divides fff. Two monic minimal polynomials divide each other and so are equal.

  2. Check the constant term. If P(0)=0P(0)=0P(0)=0, write P(t)=tQ(t)P(t)=tQ(t)P(t)=tQ(t), with QQQ monic. For α≠0\alpha\ne0α=0 this gives Q(α)=0Q(\alpha)=0Q(α)=0, again contrary to minimality.

  3. Keep the coefficients integral. If α\alphaα is an algebraic integer, take a monic integer polynomial hhh vanishing at α\alphaα (Definition 19.1). The divisibility property just proved gives P∣hP\mid hP∣h, so each root βk\beta_kβk​ of PPP is a root of hhh and is an algebraic integer.

    Over C\CC we have P(t)=∏k(t−βk)P(t)=\prod_k(t-\beta_k)P(t)=∏k​(t−βk​) with roots counted with multiplicity. Expanding, each coefficient is a sum of products of the βk\beta_kβk​, up to sign. The coefficients are algebraic integers by closure under sums and products. They are rational by definition of PPP, hence integers by the rational-integer test (Theorem 19.4).

Summary

  • The minimal polynomial of an algebraic integer has integer coefficients. Its roots are the algebraic conjugates (Definition 19.6 and Lemma 19.7).
  • Algebraic integers are the eigenvalues of integer matrices. They are closed under sums and products, and a rational one is an integer (Lemma 19.2 and Theorem 19.4).
  • On an irreducible UUU, a class sum acts by ∣K∣χU(g)/dim⁡U\abs{\clsK}\chi_U(g)/\dim U∣K∣χU​(g)/dimU, an algebraic integer because it is an eigenvalue of an integer matrix (Lemma 19.9 and Theorem 19.11).
  • Row orthonormality expresses ∣G∣/dim⁡U\abs{G}/\dim U∣G∣/dimU as a sum of products of algebraic integers. The quotient is rational, so it is an integer (Theorem 19.11). Theorem 20.6 uses the integrality of the class-sum scalar to find a proper nontrivial normal subgroup.
