2.1 Normal subgroups and quotients

In S3\SG{3}S3​ the rule xH⋅yH=xyHxH\cdot yH=xyHxH⋅yH=xyH failed for H=⟨(1 2)⟩H=\langle(1\,2)\rangleH=⟨(12)⟩ (Example 1.13), because conjugation moves HHH: (1 3)(1 2)(1 3)−1=(2 3)∉H(1\,3)(1\,2)(1\,3)^{-1}=(2\,3)\notin H(13)(12)(13)−1=(23)∈/H.

Definition 2.1 (Normal subgroup). A subgroup N≤GN\le GN≤G is normal, written N⊴GN\nsub GN⊴G, if gNg−1=NgNg^{-1}=NgNg−1=N for every g∈Gg\in Gg∈G; that is, if NG(N)=G\Norm{G}{N}=GNG​(N)=G (Corollary 1.22).

It is enough that gNg−1⊆NgNg^{-1}\subseteq NgNg−1⊆N for every ggg: applied to g−1g^{-1}g−1 this gives g−1Ng⊆Ng^{-1}Ng\subseteq Ng−1Ng⊆N, that is, N⊆gNg−1N\subseteq gNg^{-1}N⊆gNg−1. Multiplying gNg−1=NgNg^{-1}=NgNg−1=N on the right by ggg shows that normality is equivalent to gN=NggN=NggN=Ng for every ggg. Every subgroup of an abelian group is normal, and so is Z(G)\Zent{G}Z(G). The kernel of a homomorphism φ\varphiφ is normal: if φ(k)=e\varphi(k)=eφ(k)=e, then φ(gkg−1)=φ(g)φ(k)φ(g)−1=e.\varphi(gkg^{-1})=\varphi(g)\varphi(k)\varphi(g)^{-1} =e.φ(gkg−1)=φ(g)φ(k)φ(g)−1=e.

Proposition 2.2 (Quotient groups). Let H≤GH\le GH≤G.

  1. The rule xH⋅yH=xyHxH\cdot yH=xyHxH⋅yH=xyH is well defined on G/HG/HG/H if and only if H⊴GH\nsub GH⊴G. Definition 2.3 (Quotient group and quotient map). For N⊴GN\nsub GN⊴G, the group G/NG/NG/N of Proposition 2.2 is the quotient group of GGG by NNN. The homomorphism q ⁣:G→G/Nq\colon G\to G/Nq:G→G/N, g↦gNg\mapsto gNg↦gN, is the quotient map.

2.3 Direct and semidirect products

Definition 2.7 (Direct product). The direct product of groups GGG and HHH is the group G×HG\times HG×H of pairs (g,h)(g,h)(g,h), with multiplication (g,h)(g′,h′)=(gg′,hh′)(g,h)(g',h')=(gg',hh')(g,h)(g′,h′)=(gg′,hh′).

The identity is (e,e)(e,e)(e,e) and the inverse of (g,h)(g,h)(g,h) is (g−1,h−1)(g^{-1},h^{-1})(g−1,h−1). Associativity follows from that in the two factors.

For finite G,HG,HG,H, (g,h)k=e(g,h)^k=e(g,h)k=e exactly when both o(g)o(g)o(g) and o(h)o(h)o(h) divide kkk. Thus o((g,h))=lcm⁡(o(g),o(h))o((g,h))=\operatorname{lcm}(o(g),o(h))o((g,h))=lcm(o(g),o(h)). In Cm×Cn\Cyc{m}\times\Cyc{n}Cm​×Cn​, a pair of generators has order lcm⁡(m,n)\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)lcm(m,n), and every element has order dividing it. A group of order mnmnmn is cyclic exactly when it has an element of order mnmnmn. Thus the product is cyclic exactly when lcm⁡(m,n)=mn\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)=mnlcm(m,n)=mn, or equivalently gcd⁡(m,n)=1\gcd(m,n)=1gcd(m,n)=1.

Example 2.8 (Two products of cyclic groups). The pair of generators in C2×C3\Cyc{2}\times\Cyc{3}C2​×C3​ has order 666, so C2×C3≅C6,\Cyc{2}\times\Cyc{3}\cong\Cyc{6},C2​×C3​≅C6​, whereas every element of C2×C2\Cyc{2}\times\Cyc{2}C2​×C2​ has order at most 222, so that product is not cyclic.

Proposition 2.13 (Cycle decomposition). Let σ∈Sn\sigma\in\SG{n}σ∈Sn​ and a∈{1,…,n}a\in\{1,\dots,n\}a∈{1,…,n}, and let k≥1k\ge1k≥1 be least with σk(a)=a\sigma^k(a)=aσk(a)=a. Proof. Such a kkk exists, as σo(σ)=e\sigma^{o(\sigma)}=eσo(σ)=e.
