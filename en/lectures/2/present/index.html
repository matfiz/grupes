Lecture 2

Actions, cosets and orbit–stabiliser

A group can move a point in many ways. We count the points it can reach by grouping together the elements that reach the same point. This gives orbit–stabiliser and Lagrange’s theorem. We also use a faithful action to realise a group as permutations in Cayley’s theorem.

Where we are

Recording how each triangle symmetry permutes the vertices gives D3≅S3\Dih{3}\cong\SG{3}D3​≅S3​ (Example 1.12). We will record permutations in the same way for other actions. In a finite group, the order of an element is the size of its subgroup of powers (Equation (1.2)).

2.1 Actions and the points they move

A triangle symmetry sends each vertex to a vertex. A product of symmetries moves a vertex by applying its factors from right to left, as in the computation of rsrsrs in Equation (1.1). An action asks for just this rule of motion on a set.

Definition 2.1 (Action). A (left) action of a group GGG on a set XXX is a map G×X→XG\times X\to XG×X→X, (g,x)↦g⋅x(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x(g,x)↦g⋅x, such that for g,h∈Gg,h\in Gg,h∈G and x∈Xx\in Xx∈X, e⋅x=x,g⋅(h⋅x)=(gh)⋅x.e\cdot x=x,\qquad g\cdot(h\cdot x)=(gh)\cdot x.e⋅x=x,g⋅(h⋅x)=(gh)⋅x. The set XXX with this action is a GGG-set.

For example, D3\Dih{3}D3​ acts on the set of its vertices, with g⋅ig\cdot ig⋅i the vertex to which ggg sends iii. The identity leaves every label in place. The product rsrsrs sends vertex 222 to 111 because sss sends it to 333 and rrr then sends 333 to 111. This is the second action axiom at vertex 222.

The rotations rrr and r2r^2r2 move vertex 111 to vertices 222 and 333. The reflection sss leaves vertex 111 in place. We say that ggg fixes xxx when g⋅x=xg\cdot x=xg⋅x=x.

Definition 2.2 (Orbit and stabiliser). For an action of GGG on XXX and a point x∈Xx\in Xx∈X, the orbit of xxx is G⋅xG\cdot xG⋅x, and the stabiliser of xxx is Gx\Stab{G}{x}Gx​, where G⋅x={g⋅x:g∈G},Gx={g∈G:g⋅x=x}.G\cdot x=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\},\qquad \Stab{G}{x}=\{g\in G:g\cdot x=x\}.G⋅x={g⋅x:g∈G},Gx​={g∈G:g⋅x=x}. The action is transitive if there is one orbit.

Example 2.3 (D3\Dih{3}D3​ on the vertices). Under D3\Dih{3}D3​, vertex 111 can reach vertices 222 and 333 by rrr and r2r^2r2. It can also stay at 111. Its orbit is therefore {1,2,3}\{1,2,3\}{1,2,3}, and the action is transitive. From the vertex permutations in Example 1.12, only eee and sss fix vertex 111, so its stabiliser is {e,s}\{e,s\}{e,s}.

The subgroup ⟨s⟩\langle s\rangle⟨s⟩ also acts on the vertices. Its orbits are {1}\{1\}{1} and {2,3}\{2,3\}{2,3}. Changing the acting group has split one orbit into two, so this action is not transitive.

A stabiliser is a subgroup of GGG. The identity fixes xxx. If ggg and hhh both fix xxx, then ghghgh fixes xxx by the action axiom. If ggg fixes xxx, apply g−1g^{-1}g−1 on both sides of g⋅x=xg\cdot x=xg⋅x=x to obtain x=(g−1g)⋅x=g−1⋅(g⋅x)=g−1⋅x.x=(g^{-1}g)\cdot x=g^{-1}\cdot(g\cdot x)=g^{-1}\cdot x.x=(g−1g)⋅x=g−1⋅(g⋅x)=g−1⋅x. Thus the stabiliser is also closed under inverses.

The orbits partition XXX. To prove this, relate xxx and yyy when y=g⋅xy=g\cdot xy=g⋅x for some g∈Gg\in Gg∈G. We check the three properties of an equivalence relation.

  • Reflexivity. Every point is related to itself, since x=e⋅xx=e\cdot xx=e⋅x.

  • Symmetry. A move from xxx to yyy can be undone. If y=g⋅xy=g\cdot xy=g⋅x, then x=g−1⋅yx=g^{-1}\cdot yx=g−1⋅y.

  • Transitivity. Successive moves combine. If y=g⋅xy=g\cdot xy=g⋅x and z=h⋅yz=h\cdot yz=h⋅y, then z=(hg)⋅xz=(hg)\cdot xz=(hg)⋅x.

The equivalence class of xxx is the set of points related to it, which is its orbit. Transitivity of the action means that there is a single equivalence class.

A group element moves every point by a bijection because the inverse group element undoes the move. We can record an action by a map into the symmetric group SXS_XSX​ of all bijections of XXX (Definition 1.2).

Proposition 2.4 (Actions are homomorphisms to SXS_XSX​). An action of GGG on XXX gives a homomorphism ρ ⁣:G→SX\rho\colon G\to S_Xρ:G→SX​ with ρ(g)(x)=g⋅x\rho(g)(x)=g\cdot xρ(g)(x)=g⋅x. It is the intersection ker⁡ρ=⋂x∈XGx.\ker\rho=\bigcap_{x\in X}\Stab{G}{x}.kerρ=x∈X⋂​Gx​.

Proof. The action axioms give ρ(e)=id⁡\rho(e)=\idρ(e)=id and ρ(g)ρ(h)=ρ(gh)\rho(g)\rho(h)=\rho(gh)ρ(g)ρ(h)=ρ(gh). Taking h=g−1h=g^{-1}h=g−1 shows that ρ(g−1)\rho(g^{-1})ρ(g−1) inverts ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g). Thus the maps are bijections and ρ\rhoρ is a homomorphism. Conversely, the two equations hold for any homomorphism to SXS_XSX​, so they give the action axioms. A map ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) is the identity exactly when it fixes every point. This is precisely membership in every stabiliser. 

An action is faithful if its kernel is 1\triv1. Then ρ\rhoρ is injective by the kernel criterion in Remark 1.9, and G≅im⁡ρ≤SXG\cong\im\rho\le S_XG≅imρ≤SX​. For example, the action of D3\Dih{3}D3​ on its vertices is faithful. The only symmetry fixing all vertices is the identity, since two vertex vectors determine the map.

Example 2.5 (An action on a single vertex). The group ⟨s⟩\langle s\rangle⟨s⟩ fixes vertex 111. Restrict its action to the set {1}\{1\}{1}. Both elements act by the identity on this set, so the kernel is all of ⟨s⟩\langle s\rangle⟨s⟩. On the full set of vertices, sss exchanges 222 and 333, and the kernel is 1\triv1. The kernel depends on the set as well as the group.

Theorem 2.9 (Orbit–stabiliser). Let GGG act on XXX and let x∈Xx\in Xx∈X. 2.4 Lagrange and element orders

Lagrange’s theorem counts the elements of GGG by the left cosets of a subgroup HHH. To make these cosets the orbits, we need multiplication on the right by elements of HHH. The inverse in the next proof makes this a left action.

Example 2.10 (Guard: right multiplication and left actions). Try h⋅g=ghh\cdot g=ghh⋅g=gh with H=G=S3H=G=\SG{3}H=G=S3​. Starting at eee, let h=(1 2)h=(1\,2)h=(12) and h′=(1 3)h'=(1\,3)h′=(13). Applying h′h'h′ and then hhh appends them on the right in that order. The rule g↦ghg\mapsto ghg↦gh violates the left action axiom whenever HHH has a noncommuting pair.

The left cosets of HHH are disjoint and together cover GGG. Each has ∣H∣\abs{H}∣H∣ elements, as the three-coset table shows for S3\SG{3}S3​.

2.5 Cayley and left multiplication

A faithful action identifies GGG with the image of its action homomorphism to SXS_XSX​. To find such an action for every group, take X=GX=GX=G and let each element act by left multiplication.

For example, left multiplication by the triangle rotation rrr sends eee to rrr, rrr to r2r^2r2 and r2r^2r2 to eee. The same map sends sss to rsrsrs, then rsrsrs to r2sr^2sr2s, and finally r2sr^2sr2s back to sss. The points in this example are the six symmetries themselves.

Theorem 2.13 (Cayley). Every group GGG is isomorphic to a subgroup of SGS_GSG​. If ∣G∣=n\abs{G}=n∣G∣=n, it is isomorphic to a subgroup of Sn\SG{n}Sn​.

Proof. Let g⋅x=gxg\cdot x=gxg⋅x=gx for g,x∈Gg,x\in Gg,x∈G. The identity acts as the identity map, and associativity gives g⋅(h⋅x)=(gh)⋅xg\cdot(h\cdot x)=(gh)\cdot xg⋅(h⋅x)=(gh)⋅x. Thus this is a left action.

Only eee fixes eee, since g⋅e=gg\cdot e=gg⋅e=g. In particular only eee fixes every point, so the action is faithful. The associated homomorphism ρ ⁣:G→SG\rho\colon G\to S_Gρ:G→SG​ from Proposition 2.4 is therefore injective by Remark 1.9, so G≅im⁡ρ≤SGG\cong\im\rho\le S_GG≅imρ≤SG​. When GGG has nnn elements, numbering them from 111 to nnn identifies SGS_GSG​ with Sn\SG{n}Sn​. 

Cayley’s action puts D3\Dih{3}D3​ in S6\SG{6}S6​ by permuting six symmetries. The action on the triangle itself already identifies D3\Dih{3}D3​ with S3\SG{3}S3​ (Example 1.12).

2.6 Worked example: counting polygon symmetries

Example 2.14 (∣Dn∣=2n\abs{\Dih{n}}=2n∣Dn​∣=2n). Let n≥3n\ge3n≥3. We count the symmetries of the regular nnn-gon by choosing vertex 111 and applying orbit–stabiliser. Retain the rotation rrr and the reflection sss through vertex 111 from Example 1.10.

The action and orbit. The group Dn\Dih{n}Dn​ acts on the vertices because its symmetries permute them. The list. The elements rkr^krk with 0≤k<n0\le k<n0≤k<n are distinct because o(r)=no(r)=no(r)=n. The elements rksr^ksrks are also distinct by right cancellation of sss. Summary

  • A left action of GGG on XXX gives a homomorphism G→SXG\to S_XG→SX​ whose kernel consists of the elements fixing every point (Proposition 2.4). Left multiplication is faithful and gives Cayley’s embedding (Theorem 2.13).
  • The coset gGxg\Stab{G}{x}gGx​ corresponds to the point g⋅xg\cdot xg⋅x in the orbit (Theorem 2.9). For finite GGG, the orbit has size ∣G∣/∣Gx∣\abs{G}/\abs{\Stab{G}{x}}∣G∣/∣Gx​∣ (Corollary 2.11).
  • Subgroup orders and element orders divide the order of a finite group (Corollaries 2.11 and 2.12).

Thus σ\sigmaσ sends xxx to σ(x)\sigma(x)σ(x), then to σ2(x)\sigma^2(x)σ2(x), then back to xxx. These three images use up those three points as targets, so bijectivity forces σ\sigmaσ to fix the fourth point. Hence σ3=e\sigma^3=eσ3=e, contrary to the assumed order.

3. Cube rotations.

The cube has 242424 rotations. Let GGG be this group. A rotation preserves distance from the origin, so it permutes the eight vertices, the points of the cube farthest from the origin. A linear map is determined by its values on three independent vertex vectors, so GGG is finite.

A quarter-turn can carry a face to any adjacent face. A half-turn can carry it to the opposite face. Thus the action on the six faces is transitive.

A rotation preserving a face fixes its centre. It therefore fixes the axis through the origin and that centre pointwise. On the perpendicular plane it acts by a rotation, since it is orthogonal and has determinant 111. To preserve the square face, its angle must be 000, π/2\pi/2π/2, π\piπ or 3π/23\pi/23π/2. Each of these four rotations preserves the cube. The stabiliser of the face therefore has order 444, and the orbit-size formula of Corollary 2.11 gives ∣G∣=6⋅4=24.\abs{G}=6\cdot4=24.∣G∣=6⋅4=24.
